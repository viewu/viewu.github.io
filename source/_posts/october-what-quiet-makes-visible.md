### After the Noise

October began with a strange kind of freedom.

For the first few days of the National Day holiday, I woke up in the afternoon, watched movies, and let time pass without asking very much of it. There were no meetings to attend, no colleagues waiting for my work, and no immediate deadlines demanding my attention. After months filled with an internship, competitions, studying, and changes in my personal life, I finally had the opportunity to do almost nothing.

I had expected to feel relieved. Instead, I found myself somewhere between peace and emptiness.

It was not that I felt particularly sad or exhausted. There was simply a quietness I did not quite know how to interpret. When life had been moving quickly for so long, perhaps I had grown accustomed to measuring my days by the things that needed to be done. Without those obligations, I was left with a question that seemed deceptively simple: what did I actually want to do with my time?

I still went to the gym. I played basketball, spent time with friends, and occasionally returned to projects I had neglected. Even on days when I accomplished very little, exercise remained something I could rely on. There was comfort in the familiarity of it, in knowing that I could walk into the gym without feeling especially motivated and still leave having done something worthwhile.

But I was also beginning to understand that a routine, however valuable, could not answer every question about how to live.

I spent more time watching movies and thinking about books. There is something remarkable about the way stories allow us to borrow experiences we will never have ourselves. A film can take us into a stranger's family, a distant country, or a moment in history that has long since disappeared. A novel can make us care deeply about someone who has never existed.

Perhaps that is one of the reasons I have always been drawn to stories. Our own lives are inevitably limited, but our understanding of life does not have to be. Through the experiences of others, we discover possibilities that might never have occurred to us.

During October, I also returned to writing about my journey on Jeju Island. Revisiting those days of walking, the people I had encountered, and the unexpected moments along the way reminded me of something I had almost forgotten. Some of my most meaningful experiences had begun with no practical purpose beyond wanting to see what was out there.

I did not always need a career objective or a measurable achievement to justify doing something.

Sometimes curiosity was reason enough.

And perhaps that was what the quiet days at the beginning of October were trying to tell me. There were parts of life that deserved attention even when they contributed nothing obvious to my future.

### The Difference Between Doing and Knowing

As the holiday ended, my attention gradually returned to work.

I had decided to pursue multi-agent systems more seriously, hoping to turn my graduate research into something substantial. There were papers to read, experiments to reproduce, and countless technical details to understand. With AI tools assisting me, progress sometimes seemed surprisingly fast.

Yet I gradually noticed something uncomfortable: producing results was becoming easier than understanding them.

I could have AI help write code, organize experiments, and explain complicated methods. But when I looked more closely at what had been produced, I sometimes struggled to explain why a particular approach was necessary or whether the results genuinely demonstrated an improvement.

I began to realize that getting something to work was only the beginning. A more sophisticated system was not necessarily a better one, and an experiment that produced numbers did not automatically produce convincing evidence.

This reminded me of something I had learned during my first internship, which had ended shortly before October.

Real work had exposed me to problems that no tutorial could fully prepare me for. I had made mistakes, misunderstood expectations, and occasionally spent hours struggling with questions that a brief conversation might have resolved. Looking back, I could see that technical knowledge was only one part of becoming a capable engineer. The rest involved communication, responsibility, and the judgment to recognize what actually mattered.

Around the same time, I was reading about how AI might reshape employment and software engineering. The more capable these tools became, the more I wondered what it would mean to be a young engineer entering the industry.

I was excited by what AI could make possible, but I also worried about becoming dependent on its abilities without developing enough of my own.

Perhaps the challenge was no longer simply learning how to produce things, but learning how to question, evaluate, and take responsibility for what we produced.

That was the kind of ability I wanted to develop, both in research and in whatever work came next.

### A Direction Without a Deadline

Toward the end of October, another possibility appeared.

I had the opportunity to consider an internship in another city, at a company whose work was closer to some of the technologies I was interested in. It sounded promising, but accepting it would mean leaving the convenience of campus life, finding somewhere new to stay, and facing the uncertainty of another unfamiliar workplace.

Staying where I was seemed easier. I could focus on research, continue learning independently, and wait for a better opportunity. But I also knew how easily freedom could become an excuse for postponement.

I was caught between the comfort of remaining where I was and the possibility of discovering something new elsewhere. Neither choice came with any guarantee.

Perhaps this was another part of growing older: recognizing that some decisions had to be made before their consequences could be fully understood.

Meanwhile, life continued in its ordinary ways. I kept exercising, reading English articles, and thinking about returning to the piano. I wanted to spend more time with physical books, write more often, and preserve a little room for things that had no particular purpose beyond making life enjoyable.

I also found that certain memories no longer occupied the same place in my mind. Time had quietly changed my relationship with the past. Things that once seemed impossible to leave behind had gradually become easier to accept, not because they had lost their meaning, but because I no longer needed them to be different.

Looking back, October was not a particularly remarkable month. I did not achieve a major research breakthrough, transform my career, or suddenly become the disciplined person I sometimes imagined I ought to be. I spent time hesitating, revisiting old problems, and occasionally letting entire afternoons disappear.

Yet I had begun to see something differently.

There was a time when I believed staying busy was the answer to almost everything. If I felt uncertain, I learned something new. If I felt restless, I exercised. If life seemed directionless, I searched for another goal.

Action had helped me many times, and I still believed in it. But I was slowly learning that movement and direction were not always the same thing.

There would always be another technology to learn, another opportunity to pursue, another uncertainty to resolve. I did not want to spend my life chasing the next achievement while overlooking the life unfolding around me.

For now, I had my research, my unfinished projects, the people I cared about, and the quiet pleasures that made an ordinary day worth living. I had enough questions to keep me curious and enough responsibilities to keep me grounded.

I did not know exactly where the coming months would lead. Perhaps I did not need to.

October began with the unfamiliar feeling that nothing was asking anything of me.

It ended with a more difficult realization.

I would have to learn what to ask of myself.
