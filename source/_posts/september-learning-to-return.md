September feels longer than a month. Looking back at the English diaries I recorded over these weeks, I can see how often my mind changed direction. I worried about work, questioned my abilities, became confused by people, reconsidered decisions I thought I had already made, and watched something important in my life quietly change. Strangely, though, I remember September less as a month of losing things than as a month of returning.

## When the World Becomes Too Small

At the beginning of the month, uncertainty seemed to occupy almost everything. My internship was approaching an unclear ending, and I was beginning to confront questions I had avoided for a while: Was I becoming technically capable enough? Did I really want to follow the path I had imagined for myself? Should I keep pushing harder in the same direction, or was it time to explore something different?

I had always assumed that if I worked hard enough, eventually I would arrive at some clear answer about who I was supposed to become. September made that belief much harder to maintain. I began to notice how easily my sense of direction could be changed by the people around me. Someone treated me kindly, and suddenly a place felt worth staying in; someone disappointed me, and I wanted to leave. A conversation could make me confident for an afternoon, while another could make me question decisions I had spent weeks thinking about.

I realized that I am easily moved. For a while, I saw that as a weakness, but now I think the problem is not that I am sensitive to people. I actually like that part of myself. I like noticing people, having conversations that unexpectedly become deep, and discovering how someone else sees the world. The real problem begins when every emotion becomes a new direction.

There has to be something quieter underneath all of that movement—a center. I am still trying to find out what mine is, but maybe that is partly what growing up means: slowly learning which voices around you deserve to be heard without allowing every one of them to become your own.

One evening, in the middle of all this thinking, a friend called me to play basketball. We played for almost two hours. Nothing important happened, and somehow something important did.

For several days, I had been living almost entirely inside my own mind. Work, people, decisions, and the future seemed to grow larger the longer I looked at them. Then suddenly I was running across a court, chasing the ball, laughing, breathing hard, and responding to people around me instead of thoughts inside me. The world became physical again.

![The basketball court at dusk, where the world became physical again](/images/september-learning-to-return/3.webp)

I remembered that there was still basketball, still the gym, still music, still friends returning to campus, still bicycles to ride, things to learn, places to go, and conversations that had not happened yet. The problem had not disappeared; life had simply become larger than the problem.

That may have been one of the most important realizations of the month. I have always been comfortable being alone. I need solitude because it gives me room to think, write, train, learn, and understand myself. But solitude and isolation are not the same thing. Stay inside your own mind for too long and the world slowly becomes smaller, until your problems begin to look like the whole landscape. Other people expand it again.

## Learning to Reach Out

This became even clearer through several conversations during September. I have a habit of trying to solve things internally. If I am confused by someone, I analyze them. If something hurts, I think about it alone. If I decide to step away, I often do so quietly, without explaining much. For a long time, I thought this was independence.

But several times this month, a single honest conversation gave me more clarity than days of thinking. Not because conversation always produces an answer—it often does not—but because sometimes it takes something that has been circling endlessly inside your head and places it in the space between two people. Somehow, it becomes lighter there.

![Two chairs facing the sunset, the space between two people](/images/september-learning-to-return/2.webp)

I began to understand that being independent does not mean remaining silent. Asking for help does not make me weaker, and reaching toward people does not mean I cannot stand on my own. In fact, communication may be one of the abilities I have underestimated in myself. I like talking to people. I can listen, connect with very different personalities, and stay curious about lives that look nothing like mine. Maybe instead of retreating whenever something becomes difficult, I should learn to use that ability more deliberately.

September eventually gave me a reason to practice this. Something I had imagined as part of my future changed, and for a while I did what I usually do: I thought about it from every possible angle. I tried to understand what had happened, what I could have done differently, what reality had changed, and whether there was still something left to repair.

Eventually, I decided that thinking quietly was no longer enough, so I spoke. I said what I needed to say and tried once, honestly. After that, something inside me became unexpectedly calm—not because I received the answer I wanted, but because, for once, I had not disappeared into silence.

There is a particular kind of regret that comes from losing something, but there is another kind that comes from knowing you never really tried to reach for it. I think I was more afraid of the second. Once I had spoken, the future was no longer entirely mine to control. That was painful, but also strangely freeing.

Some things can be understood without being repaired. Some things can matter deeply without lasting forever, and some endings do not require us to decide that everything before them was a mistake. I am still learning how to accept that.

## The Road Back

For a short time, I also felt the familiar urge to turn every disappointment into ambition: work harder, become better, become stronger, make more money, build a better life. There is something seductive about that story. Pain becomes fuel, and one day your success becomes proof that nothing could defeat you.

But the more I thought about it, the less I wanted to live that way. Growth driven entirely by the desire to prove something is still a kind of dependence, because you are still allowing the past to choose your direction.

I would rather become better because I am curious. I want to understand technology more deeply because I genuinely enjoy learning how things work. I want to explore AI agents and perhaps, one day, embodied intelligence because I want to see where those ideas lead. I want to become physically stronger because I love using my body, and I want financial independence because freedom matters to me. I want to write because language helps me notice things I would otherwise miss, and I want to meet more people because every person seems to contain a version of the world I have never seen.

That feels like a healthier kind of ambition: not becoming impossible to hurt, but becoming someone who can be hurt without losing himself.

By the end of September, many of the questions I began the month with were still unanswered. I still do not know exactly what kind of work I will eventually be best at, where I will live years from now, which people will stay in my life, or which version of myself I will become. But I am beginning to think that uncertainty is not always a problem waiting to be eliminated. Sometimes it is simply the condition of being alive.

There are moments when we should make a decision, moments when we should speak, and moments when the best thing we can do is keep living while the answer slowly takes shape. Go to the gym. Play basketball. Call a friend. Read. Work. Learn something difficult. Make something. Sit somewhere quiet and write. Let life continue expanding around whatever currently feels enormous.

When I read my diaries from this month, I notice the same pattern appearing again and again: I become confused, move too far into my own head, something pulls me outward, and eventually I return.

Maybe resilience is not the ability to remain untouched by life. I do not think I want that kind of strength anymore. Maybe resilience is simply knowing the road back after something has shaken you—back to your friends, your body, your curiosity, your work, your music, the world, and finally yourself.

![A bench and a notebook at dusk, on the road back](/images/september-learning-to-return/1.webp)

September began with uncertainty, and it ended with uncertainty too. But I am no longer standing inside it in quite the same way.

I used to think peace would come when I finally figured everything out. Now I think peace may be something much simpler: knowing that not everything has to be figured out before life can continue.

Some things ended this month. Other things are only beginning.

And somewhere between the two, I am learning how to return.
